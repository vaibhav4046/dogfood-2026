# Judging

How a submission becomes a number, and what the organizer can argue with.

Implementation: `src/lib/normalize.js` (mathematics) and
`src/services/judging.js` (aggregation). Worked numbers on the real fixtures:
[`normalization-proof.md`](normalization-proof.md), which is generated by
`scripts/normalization-proof.js` and contains nothing typed by hand.

---

## 1. Raw scoring

The defensible baseline, and the only thing T2 requires.

For each completed review, a weighted mean of the criterion scores:

```
review_score   = Σ (weight_k / Σ weight) * x_k
project_score  = mean(review_score for each review of that project)
```

Weights are relative and normalised to sum to 1 at aggregation time, so an
organizer editing them does not have to hit a magic number. If every weight is
zero the API refuses the change (`validation_failed`) and the aggregator falls
back to equal shares rather than dividing by zero. Both paths are tested.

A criterion the judge left blank is **skipped, not scored as zero**. A missing
answer and a zero answer are different things.

Ranks are competition ranks: 1, 2, 2, 4. A genuine tie is shown as a tie rather
than broken by a tiebreak nobody asked for.

## 2. The problem with raw scoring

Five judges rate the same forty projects. One gives 4–5 across the board, one
gives 2–3, one gives everything exactly 3, one alternates 1 and 5.

Averaging those numbers measures the judges as much as the projects. On the
fixture data, judge means span **3.000 to 4.222** on a 1–5 scale — a spread of
over a point, which is larger than a lot of the differences between the projects
being judged.

The controlled case, in `scripts/measure-distortion.js`: **one submission**,
judged once by two lenient judges and once by two harsh judges.

| | lenient panel | harsh panel | gap |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| raw | 5.0000 | 1.0000 | **4.0000** |
| normalized | 3.8571 | 2.1429 | **1.7143** |

Identical work, separated by four points and five ranks under raw scoring.
Normalization removes **57.1%** of that gap.

## 3. Normalization

### Per judge, per criterion

Over the reviews that judge actually completed:

```
mu_jk    = mean of judge j's scores on criterion k
sd_jk    = population standard deviation of those scores
n_j      = number of completed reviews by judge j
lambda_j = n_j / (n_j + K)                 K = 4
```

### Per individual score

```
z_ij     = (x_ij - mu_jk) / sd_jk
z'_ij    = lambda_j * z_ij                 shrinkage
x~_ij    = clamp( global_mean + z'_ij * global_sd, min_k, max_k )
```

`global_mean` and `global_sd` are taken over every completed review on that
criterion. Mapping back onto the global distribution rather than onto the
rubric's midpoint keeps the result on a scale a judge can look at and argue
with, instead of an abstract z.

The project score is the weighted mean of `x~` over its reviews, using the same
weights as raw.

### Why `K = 4`

A judge's *own* standard deviation is a noisy estimate. With 4 reviews,
`lambda = 0.5`: half of their measured spread is trusted, half is pulled toward
the population. At 20 reviews, `lambda = 0.83`. At 1 review, `lambda = 0.2`.

Four is not a tuned constant. It is the point at which a judge's own spread is
as trustworthy as the population's — a 50% shrinkage, stated in one number that
is printed next to every judge's severity in the Calibration Lab. A judge who
disagrees with the panel sees their `lambda` and knows exactly how much the
model is discounting them.

## 4. Edge cases, and what the code actually does

### A judge with no spread (`sd ≈ 0`)

The fixtures contain one: `Iva Petrova`, 3 reviews, `sd = 0.000`.

The division is undefined, so it is **never performed**. The score is kept
exactly as given, the review is flagged `zero-variance-judge`, and the judge's
`degenerate` flag is set.

This is the one place where a "clever" implementation would do damage. A judge
who rates everything 3 carries no relative information; dividing by a near-zero
sd turns their flat 3 into a large arbitrary number, and a project they happened
to be assigned lands wherever that noise pointed. Their review is neither
dropped nor inflated.

Note the distinction from a judge who is *partly* constant: `Sana Aziz` has an
overall sd of 1.105 but is constant on `functionality`. `degenerate` is
therefore only true when **every** criterion has no spread, and
`constantCriteria` names which ones. The first implementation checked only the
first criterion and the generated proof showed "no spread" beside an sd of 1.105.

### A judge with fewer than two reviews on a criterion

There is no personal baseline to estimate, so there is no severity to remove.
The score is standardized against the **global** distribution instead, then
shrunk by the same `lambda` — which is small, because `n` is small. Flagged
`insufficient-judge-history`.

The first implementation kept the raw score. That was wrong and the unit test
that caught it says so: it let the *least* trustworthy judge count at full
weight, which is the opposite of what shrinkage is for.

### A project with two reviews next to one with five

Both are ranked. `review_count` and `judge_count` travel with every row into
the CSV and the Calibration Lab, so a thin result is visibly thin rather than
quietly equal to a well-reviewed one. There is no minimum-review threshold that
silently excludes a project — a project with one review is ranked, and marked.

### A genuine tie

Competition ranks (1, 2, 2, 4). Shown as a tie.

### No completed reviews at all

The Calibration Lab reports `mode: none` and says so, rather than rendering an
empty table that looks like zero scores.

### Determinism

No randomness, no wall-clock input, no iteration-order dependence.
`judgeStats` is sorted by id before hashing, because it is built by walking a
Map in first-appearance order and a fingerprint that moves when you re-sort the
input is not a fingerprint.

Asserted in `tests/unit/normalize.test.js`:

- same input → same fingerprint
- one changed review → different fingerprint
- **reversed input → same fingerprint and same ranks**

`fingerprint` is a SHA-256 over the ranked output, truncated to 16 hex chars,
published in the Calibration Lab and printed in the proof. For the fixture data
it is `e581a615082f0338`.

## 5. What the organizer sees

The Calibration Lab (`/organizer`, organizer-only) shows, side by side:

- **Judge severity** — n, mean, sd, `lambda`, and why a judge is flagged
  (`no spread at all — kept raw`, `constant on functionality`, `thin sample,
  shrunk`). Sorted harshest-last by mean.
- **Rank movement** — every project whose rank differs between raw and
  normalized, with the direction and size.
- **Raw ranking** and **normalized ranking** as two separate tables.
- The global mean, global sd, and the result fingerprint.

Nothing is hidden and nothing is toggled by default: both rankings are always
on screen. The organizer's decision is which one to publish, and
`POST /api/organizer/publish` records that choice in the audit log.

## 6. What this does not do

Stated plainly, because a normalization scheme that claims too much is worse
than none.

- **It does not change a judge's raw score.** Normalization is a *view* over the
  reviews, published alongside the raw one.
- **It does not reorder a project on the strength of one review.** Shrinkage is
  exactly what prevents that.
- **It does not detect collusion.** Two judges who copy each other will produce
  a high correlation and two low-variance profiles, and the Calibration Lab will
  show both — but nothing flags the pair. That is a gap, and it is listed in
  [`THREAT-MODEL.md`](THREAT-MODEL.md).
- **It does not correct for criterion bias asymmetry well.** If one judge is
  lenient on functionality and harsh on quality, both are corrected
  independently, which is right; but the *interaction* between criteria is not
  modelled.
- **It does not weight by judge expertise.** A judge who has never been
  calibrated is treated exactly like one who has, apart from the `n` shrinkage.
  Real systems weight by agreement with the panel over time; that would need
  historical data this platform does not have yet.

## 7. Future work, not attempted

**Pairwise judging with a Bradley-Terry model.** If every judge ranked every
project, the aggregate is already a sensible answer. Pairwise comparison is
stronger when the field is large and individual reviews are noisy, because each
comparison is easier to make than a full ranking.

It is not attempted here, and the spec is right that a half-built version is
worse than none. The pieces it would need are all present — `results` can hold
a third mode, `computeResults` is already a pure function of the reviews, and
the fingerprint discipline already applies.

The honest reason it is worth building: with 41 projects and 126 reviews, judges
compare inconsistently across the field. Bradley-Terry would estimate latent
ability per judge and per project from pairwise outcomes and expose confidence
intervals, which this model deliberately does not claim to produce.

**Judge calibration over time** — weighting by historical agreement with the
panel — needs several events' worth of data, which is a platform-lifetime
question, not a build-phase one.
